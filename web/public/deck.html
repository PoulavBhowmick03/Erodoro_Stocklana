Lock a tokenized share, cap your upside at a strike you choose, and sell that upside to someone else for cash today.

Value of one locked share to its owner, against price at maturity

Not deployed to mainnet. Nothing here holds real value.

01  The problem

A tokenized share sits there and pays you nothing.

You hold 1 TSLAx — a Tesla share that lives on Solana, worth $400. Most of the time it will not reach $500 in three months, and you will have been paid nothing for the wait.

In traditional markets you would sell that unreached upside as a covered call and pocket the premium. That instrument needs a broker, an options desk, and a counterparty who can find you. None of that reaches a token sitting in your wallet.

Holding it plain — every dollar yours, and none of it paid up front

02  What it does

Lock the share. Split it in two. Sell one half.

Lock

Deposit 1 TSLAx, pick a strike of $500 and a maturity three months out. The vault mints you two tokens against it.

P — keep

The share, capped at the strike. This is the half you hold.

N — sell

Everything above the strike. Sell it for maybe $15. That cash is yours immediately, whatever happens next.

In traditional terms the P holder has written a covered call and the N holder owns it. Both halves are ordinary tokens and trade independently. Hand back equal amounts of each and the original share comes straight back out.

04  The part that must not be glossed over

This is not downside protection.

Below the strike the P holder takes the full fall, exactly as they would holding the share. If it goes to $250 you lose $150. The $15 premium is the only cushion. You have sold your upside, not bought insurance.

Nothing catches it

Any interface implying a floor — “protected”, “safe”, “hedged” — would be false, and the first person to read the contract would say so publicly.

05  How it works — the lifecycle

Five instructions, and nobody who can pick the price.

InstructionWhoWhat it does
splitAnyone Locks collateral, mints equal P and N.
mergeAnyone Burns equal amounts of both, returns the share. Works while paused, so a pause never traps collateral behind existing claims.
settleAnyone, once Reads a signed Pyth price and freezes the two pools. Permissionless: anyone can poke it, nobody chooses the number.
redeem_pP holders Pro-rata claim on the frozen pool for that side.
redeem_nN holders 
-- the whole product, in three lines
price <= strike:  p_pool = collateral                      n_pool = 0
price >  strike:  p_pool = floor(collateral*strike/price)  n_pool = collateral - p_pool
payout          = floor(amount * pool / supply_at_settlement)

Payouts floor, so total paid never exceeds collateral locked, in any redemption order. Where rounding moves value between the sides it moves it to P, the senior claim — asserted in tests rather than left to emerge.

06  How it works — the programs

Four programs and a shared math crate.

series

The vault and the settlement engine. Holds the collateral, owns the two mints, splits the pool at maturity.

oracle‑adapter

Owns the FeedConfig accounts that make a feed's identity tamper‑evident. Linked as a library, not a CPI target — the price account is in the transaction anyway.

factory

Policy bounds, allowlists, and the canonical registry. Creation is permissionless; the registry decides what is canonical.

market

An order book for the two legs, with an ephemeral-rollup hot path. A prototype.

No admin can touch active collateral. The series admin can pause and unpause splits, and sweep dust once both supplies are zero — that is the entire surface. It cannot settle, cannot change a term, and can be renounced permanently.

The factory has no power over a series it created, not even to pause one. Front ends must list from the registry's SeriesRecord, never from the series program's accounts at large.

07  Why it is not trivial

The issuer keeps five levers over anything in the vault.

The collateral is a Token-2022 mint. This is not a protocol whose security can exceed the issuer's honesty, and the design says so rather than papering over it.

LeverWhat the issuer can doWhat the protocol does
permanentDelegate Move or burn collateral out of a vault, at any time, with no consent. Redemption prices the shortfall proportionally instead of paying first‑come.
scaledUiAmount Rebase every balance for a corporate action. The strike is adjusted by the same ratio at settlement.
transferHook Arm a blocklist without redeploying the mint. Every collateral movement resolves hook accounts from day one.
pausableConfig Halt all transfers globally. Transfers revert loudly; settle still works, so a pause delays redemption rather than losing it.
freeze authority Freeze the vault's token account. Same as a pause, scoped to one series. 
-- a drained vault, priced rather than gated
available = vault_balance
expected  = (p_pool - p_redeemed) + (n_pool - n_redeemed)
payout    = floor(quoted * available / expected)   when available < expected

Verified on a validator with a real permanent delegate: two holders, unequal arrival order, half the vault seized after settlement. First and last redeemer on each side are paid within 2 raw units of each other.

08  The bug that looks boring

A stock split would silently wipe out N.

A 2‑for‑1 split halves the price to $300 and doubles every balance. A naive contract compares $300 against the stored $500 strike, concludes “below strike”, and hands everything to P — destroying N in an event where nobody lost a penny. 

-- resolved at the price timestamp, never the clock
effective_strike = ceil(strike * m_creation / m_settlement)

The stored strike is never mutated, both multipliers are recorded so any payout can be audited afterwards, and creation refuses a series that would span a corporate action already scheduled.

Post-split, the price lands at $300 — right of the adjusted strike, left of the stored one

Verified on a validator: a 2‑for‑1 split landing mid‑series leaves both pools bit‑identical to the unsplit case. The dividend case is not solved — a rebased cash dividend is not a clean ratio, and no on‑chain field distinguishes it from a split.

09  Settling on a real price

Tesla trades weekdays. The token trades always.

A price at 3am on a Sunday is not a market price, it is noise on a thin book — and settling real money against it would be a gift to whoever nudged it. The design assumed Pyth publishes a trading status to gate on. It does not: the account the Solana receiver actually writes carries price, confidence, exponent and publish time, and nothing about whether the market was open. 

-- so the requirement is met structurally instead
accept a quote only if  publish_time ∈ [ maturity_ts , maturity_ts + max_price_lag ]

-- and refuse a tuple where no quote could ever qualify
settlement_delay <= max_price_lag + min(series_max_age, feed_max_age)

Maturity at a real close

The window is computed off-chain per maturity and frozen at creation, keeping trading calendars, holidays and daylight-saving out of the program.

Feed identity is immutable

The feed id cannot be changed after creation. Only the account an update is read from can rotate, and the embedded id is checked on every read.

Signatures have a floor

A partially verified update below the configured signature count is rejected. A one-signature update is cheap to post and correspondingly cheap to forge.

10  Why an ephemeral rollup

A maker who cannot reprice will not quote.

The premium is the P holder's entire compensation, and it only exists if somebody can profitably quote N. An options maker reprices as spot moves — hundreds of updates an hour against very little volume. At a fee and 400ms each, the spread needed to break even is wider than the premium is worth.

So exactly one account is delegated: the Book for a leg, holding orders and per-trader balances together. Everything that holds value stays on Solana. Placing, cancelling and filling take no token accounts at all — they move entries in a ledger, and no token moves until somebody withdraws on L1.

Measured on a local stackLatency
Place an order in the rollup7–17 ms
Fill an order in the rollup6–12 ms
Commit accepted5–20 ms
Committed state visible on L1~510 ms
Undelegate round trip~515 ms

The gap between those two groups is the whole argument for the split. Quoting is instant; touching the base layer costs half a second, and a maker repricing a hundred times a minute can afford exactly one of those.

The two scenarios the design exists for are both verified on a validator against a real Token-2022 mint: the split that would have wiped out N, and the drain of a settled vault that pays every redeemer the same haircut.

12  What is not solved

The contract works with zero users. The product does not.

Your entire compensation for capping your upside is that $15, and it only exists if somebody actually wants to buy N. Nobody has committed yet. That is not an engineering problem and no amount of code fixes it.

N liquidity

Unsolved, and the real risk to the product. Seeding a maker, a Dutch auction at creation, or an existing options desk — all commercial decisions, none evaluated.

Dividends

A rebased cash dividend moves value from P to N, the wrong way, and nothing on-chain distinguishes it from a split. Creation refuses scheduled changes; that is the conservative half of an answer.

The market program

A prototype with unresolved accounting and initialization findings. Deployment is not evidence.

Mainnet

Not deployed. Series and factory are not even on devnet — together they need about 5.9 SOL of rent and the faucet is capped.
